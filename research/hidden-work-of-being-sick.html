The hidden work of being sick

We asked 400 adults living with chronic conditions how much time care takes, which tasks they dread, and what they want someone else to do. They asked for action.

Summary

Being sick comes with a second job. There are bills to dispute, forms to fax, appointments to line up, and portals that do not talk to each other. Nobody trains for it, and almost nobody measures it.

In January 2026 a research partner surveyed 400 U.S. adults who were managing at least one chronic condition. We wanted numbers for something we had heard in thousands of conversations with patients and caregivers: that information was rarely the problem, and the work was.

Three things stood out. The time was larger than planning models tend to assume. The confusion was steady and everyday, not a one-off crisis. And when people were asked to choose between being informed and being helped, they chose help. The people managing the most conditions chose it most strongly.

Who we asked

Four hundred people, split by how much they manage

Half of the respondents were managing one or two conditions. The other half were managing three to five. The even split let us compare a simpler journey with a complicated one.

The sample skewed middle-aged and modest in income. Seven in ten households earned under $100,000 a year. That matters when reading the rest of this paper: these answers did not come from a group with money and time to spare (Figure 1).

Part I

The hours nobody counts

We asked how much time people spent each month on three kinds of work around their care: learning about their condition, dealing with insurance and bills, and handling scheduling and paperwork.

The answers added up to about 5.8 hours a month. More than half of respondents spent two hours or more on learning alone. The three kinds of work stacked on top of each other. Time spent on one did not come out of another (Figure 2).

The total was almost the same whether someone managed one condition or five. That surprised us. A simpler diagnosis did not buy back the time.

Part II

Lost, most days

Hours are one measure. The other is how the work feels. We asked people whether problems finding their way through care had cost them, and whether they felt lost.

The sample

Start with everyone.

Each dot is one of the 400 people who answered. Keep scrolling and the dots will light up as people agree with a statement.

Q18

Nearly half had lost time or money to the system.

45% agreed that problems navigating care had caused waste. 14% agreed strongly. "Waste" is an abstract word, and almost half the sample still recognised it in their own care.

Q19

Almost as many felt lost.

43% agreed that they felt lost navigating healthcare. 15% agreed strongly. These were people already in care, with a diagnosis and a doctor.

The shape of it

Most of it was "somewhat".

The largest share chose the milder answer. We read that as a steady, everyday confusion and not a single emergency. It is the kind of problem a pamphlet does not fix, because it comes back next month.

One result ran against our expectation. People with one or two conditions agreed slightly more often than people with three to five (Figure 4). One reading is that people with more conditions have had longer to learn the system. We did not test that, and the gap is small.

Part III

The task people dread is the one they want help with

We asked two separate questions. Which tasks do you least like doing? And which would you most want help with? Bills and insurance came first on both lists, in both groups (Figure 5).

The tasks tied to money and paperwork outranked the clinical ones. Help with a second opinion or a clinical trial sat at the bottom of the list. That does not make those things unimportant. It means that for this group, the daily weight was administrative.

The open-text answers said the same thing in fewer words.

Part IV

Given 100 points, people spent the most on action

The clearest result came from a forced choice. We gave each person 100 points to divide between three kinds of help: someone taking action on their behalf, access to their medical records, and education tailored to them.

Action won, with 39.9 points. Among people managing three to five conditions it rose to 42.8 (Figure 6).

Records and education still mattered. Together they took 60 of the 100 points. But neither was what people reached for first. A person waiting on hold with their insurer does not want an article about how insurance works. They want the call made.

The finding in one line

People were not asking to understand the system better. They were asking for someone to deal with it.

Part V

Would people use a navigator, and one that runs on AI?

We asked people to rate their interest in a care navigation service from 1 to 10. About half answered 8 or higher, and roughly one in four chose 10. Interest was higher among people managing more conditions.

We then asked about navigation delivered mainly by AI. We expected a drop. There was very little: 45% rated it 8 or higher (Figure 7).

We hold this result loosely. Saying you are interested in a survey is not the same as trusting a service with your insurance appeal. What it told us was narrower: AI was not a reason for people to turn away, as long as the work got done.

What we did with it

We changed the order we built things in

These results set our build order. We started with the work people dreaded most and wanted help with most, and left the thing they asked for least until last.

  1. Bills and insuranceFinancial aid found and filed. Denials challenged.
  2. Scheduling and paperworkAppointments booked and moved. Forms filled and sent.
  3. RecordsEvery portal connected into one place.
  4. EducationUseful, and built last, because it was asked for least.

It also changed how Arul talks to people. Arul does the task and reports back, and patients approve anything sent in their name. You can read how that works in Access to Care.

Method and limits

How this was done, and what it cannot tell you

Design
Quantitative survey with open-text questions
When
January 2026
Who
400 U.S. adults managing one or more chronic conditions
Groups
200 with 1 to 2 conditions; 200 with 3 to 5
Measures
Hours per month, agreement scales, task ratings, a 100-point allocation, 1 to 10 interest ratings
Reported
Means and shares from the survey's selected results

Limits

  • It was not independent. The survey was designed and run by a research partner with a stake in Arul's work. Read it as a strong signal, not as third-party evidence.
  • It was a broad chronic-condition sample. Only 13% of respondents had cancer. The results may not hold for people in cancer treatment or for caregivers, who were not surveyed separately.
  • Time was self-reported. People estimated their own hours. Nobody kept a diary.
  • We report selected results. We worked from the analysis shared with us, which did not include every response distribution. Where a figure leaves something out, its note says so.
  • No significance tests are reported. Differences between the two groups of 200 are descriptive. Small gaps, like the one in Figure 4, should be treated with care.
  • Interest is not use. Ratings of interest describe what people said, not what they went on to do.
